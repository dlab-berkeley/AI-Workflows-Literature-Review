# AI Assisted Literature Review

<p class="subtitle">UC Berkeley D-Lab</p>

Note:
Welcome participants and ask them to keep one focused research question in mind. The workshop is about using AI to extend and organize a review while keeping disciplinary judgment visible.

---

## What is a literature review for?

Coverage, positioning, theory building, method comparison, or something else?

Note:
Open with discussion. Ask two or three participants from different fields to describe what a literature review needs to accomplish in their discipline. Use their answers to show why no single AI workflow fits every review.

---

## AI can help before it can conclude

AI can help discover, screen, compare, and organize. Researchers still decide what matters and what the evidence supports.

Note:
Frame the session as disciplined tool use. The tools can accelerate early work, but the review still depends on framing, source evaluation, interpretation, and disciplinary context.

---

## Four tools, different jobs

| Tool | Useful for | Check before trusting it |
|---|---|---|
| **Litmaps** | citation relationships and related papers | whether the map covers the field you need |
| **Elicit** | semantic search and early screening | whether papers and extracted fields match the source |
| **Consensus** | cited answers to focused questions | what methods, populations, and disagreements the summary hides |
| **General AI** | search terms, screening questions, and note organization | whether every claim has paper-grounded support |

Note:
Introduce the tools as different supports for different steps. Do not present any one of them as a complete literature review.

---

## Litmaps

1. Begin with one or two seed papers
2. Trace citation relationships
3. Inspect central and peripheral papers
4. Ask what the map leaves out

The map shows connections. It does not decide which scholarship matters.

Note:
Use one seed paper from a participant or prepared example. Ask what appears central, peripheral, or unexpectedly connected. Then ask what the map cannot tell the group without reading the papers.

## Consensus

> A cited answer is a starting point for inspection.

Ask:

1. Do the cited studies address the same population?
2. Do they use comparable methods and definitions?
3. Does the summary preserve important disagreement?

Note:
Use a focused research question and read past the headline answer. Invite the room to challenge the word consensus by asking whose evidence, which methods, and what context the label includes.

---

## General AI assistants

| Useful work | Human checks |
|---|---|
| generate search terms and disciplinary variations | confirm that sources exist |
| create screening questions | check relevance against the research question |
| organize verified notes into a synthesis outline | identify flattened nuance or disagreement |
| translate notes into plainer language | verify meaning and citation support |

Note:
Use a general model after you have verified notes or source text. Explain why fluent writing can make unsupported claims feel more credible than they are.

## A synthesis keeps differences visible

  <div class="msg user">Summarize what these papers say.</div>
  <div class="msg bot">The studies generally find a positive effect.</div>
  <div class="msg user">Separate the findings by population, method, and definition. Name disagreements and missing evidence before summarizing.</div>
  <div class="msg bot">The result differs across populations and measures. Two studies do not support a broad conclusion. One population is absent from the evidence set.</div>
</div>

Note:
The second prompt asks for the structure that a responsible synthesis needs. The point is not the wording of the example answer. The point is to keep differences visible before writing a general claim.

---

## The synthesis lab

Use a focused question from your own discipline.

1. Search with at least two tools
2. Keep links or citations to the papers
3. Compare what each tool surfaced
4. Record uncertainty and missing evidence
5. List what you still need to read yourself

Note:
Give participants 20 minutes. They may work alone or in pairs. Circulate and ask what each tool decided or omitted without making that choice explicit.

---

## Synthesis worksheet

| Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| What does the literature appear to say? | |
| What remains uncertain or underspecified? | |
| Where did the tools disagree? | |
| Why might their result sets differ? | |
| What still needs human checking? | |

Note:
Participants can write in a separate document or on paper. Ask them to distinguish a tentative pattern from a claim they would feel comfortable putting into a literature review.

---

## Prompts for the activity

```text
Suggest stronger search questions, synonyms, and disciplinary
variations for this topic.

What papers, methods, or populations may be absent from this
result set?

Create a synthesis outline from these verified notes. Label
claims that still need checking.

What disagreements should I examine before describing consensus?
```

Note:
Participants do not need to use every prompt. Ask them to select the prompt that addresses the weakest part of their current workflow.

---

## Questions for critique

1. Which tool seemed most convincing, and why?
2. What would have been easy to miss with only one tool?
3. Where did a tool help you think better?
4. Where did it tempt you to stop thinking?

Note:
During the share-out, compare review habits as much as results. Invite participants to explain how they checked a claim, noticed a gap, or changed their question.

---

## What remains human?

Framing the question. Reading the evidence. Understanding context. Interpreting disagreement. Taking responsibility for the claims you write.

**AI can accelerate a literature review. It cannot replace disciplinary judgment.**

Note:
Close by asking what responsible synthesis requires when tools can summarize papers faster than people can read them. Ask each participant to keep one prompt, question, or mini workflow for their own practice.
