# AI Assisted Literature Review

<p class="subtitle">UC Berkeley D-Lab</p>

Note:
Welcome participants and ask them to keep one focused research question in mind. The workshop is about using AI to extend and organize a review while keeping disciplinary judgment visible.

---

## What is a literature review for?

Coverage, positioning, theory building, method comparison, or something else?

Note:
Open with discussion. Ask two or three participants from different fields to describe what a literature review needs to accomplish in their discipline. Use their answers to show why no single AI workflow fits every review.

---

## AI can help before it can conclude

AI can help discover, screen, compare, and organize. Researchers still decide what matters and what the evidence supports.

Note:
Frame the session as disciplined tool use. The tools can accelerate early work, but the review still depends on framing, source evaluation, interpretation, and disciplinary context.

---

## Four tools, different jobs

| Tool | Useful for | Check before trusting it |
|---|---|---|
| **Litmaps** | citation relationships and related papers | whether the map covers the field you need |
| **Elicit** | semantic search and early screening | whether papers and extracted fields match the source |
| **Consensus** | cited answers to focused questions | what methods, populations, and disagreements the summary hides |
| **General AI** | search terms, screening questions, and note organization | whether every claim has paper-grounded support |

Note:
Introduce the tools as different supports for different steps. Do not present any one of them as a complete literature review.

---

## Litmaps

1. Begin with one or two seed papers
2. Trace citation relationships
3. Inspect central and peripheral papers
4. Ask what the map leaves out

The map shows connections. It does not decide which scholarship matters.

Note:
Use one seed paper from a participant or prepared example. Ask what appears central, peripheral, or unexpectedly connected. Then ask what the map cannot tell the group without reading the papers.

## Consensus

> A cited answer is a starting point for inspection.

Ask:

1. Do the cited studies address the same population?
2. Do they use comparable methods and definitions?
3. Does the summary preserve important disagreement?

Note:
Use a focused research question and read past the headline answer. Invite the room to challenge the word consensus by asking whose evidence, which methods, and what context the label includes.

---

## General AI assistants

| Useful work | Human checks |
|---|---|
| generate search terms and disciplinary variations | confirm that sources exist |
| create screening questions | check relevance against the research question |
| organize verified notes into a synthesis outline | identify flattened nuance or disagreement |
| translate notes into plainer language | verify meaning and citation support |

Note:
Use a general model after you have verified notes or source text. Explain why fluent writing can make unsupported claims feel more credible than they are.

## A synthesis keeps differences visible

<div class="chat">
  <div class="msg user">Summarize what these papers say.</div>
  <div class="msg bot">The studies generally find a positive effect.</div>
  <div class="msg user">Separate the findings by population, method, and definition. Name disagreements and missing evidence before summarizing.</div>
  <div class="msg bot">The result differs across populations and measures. Two studies do not support a broad conclusion. One population is absent from the evidence set.</div>
</div>

Note:
The second prompt asks for the structure that a responsible synthesis needs. The point is not the wording of the example answer. The point is to keep differences visible before writing a general claim.

## Purpose

Use a question from your own discipline to:

1. Compare AI-assisted research tools
2. Verify claims against original sources
3. Draft a synthesis that identifies what helps, what the evidence suggests, and what the evidence leaves out

Note:
State the workshop purpose before introducing the activity. Emphasize that the goal is not to find one winning tool. The goal is to build a defensible workflow that connects tool output to original sources and researcher judgment.

---

## Learning outcomes

By the end, you will be able to:

1. Match Litmaps, Elicit, Consensus, and general AI assistants to different research tasks
2. Compare findings across at least two tools
3. Verify one claim in an original PDF with a page number and supporting passage or paraphrase
4. Develop an AI-assisted synthesis that includes disagreement and limitations

Note:
Preview the four outcomes. Tell fellows that the lab deliverable will give them evidence of each outcome: a tool comparison, a verified claim, and a short synthesis.

## The board workflow

| Step | Research decision |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Focused question |
| 2 | Broad discovery |
| 3 | Inclusion and exclusion screening |
| 4 | Keep list |
| 5 | Reading plan and annotation |
| 6 | Evidence matrix |
| 7 | Synthesis memo or presentation |

Note:
Build this sequence on the board and keep it visible. Return to it during each demo so fellows can locate what the tool is helping with and what human decision follows.

---

## Research tools across the workflow

| Tool | Role in workflow | Human in the loop |
|---|---|---|
| **Litmaps** | discover papers through citation relationships | choose seeds and inspect coverage gaps |
| **Elicit** | search, screen, and extract candidate evidence | verify relevance and extracted fields |
| **Consensus** | explore cited answers to focused questions | inspect methods, populations, and disagreement |
| **ChatGPT or Claude** | refine questions, terms, criteria, and synthesis structure | provide verified material and check every claim |
| **Claude or Codex with a configured research connector or MCP** | bring tool retrieval into one working conversation | inspect provenance and verify the original source |

Note:
Ask fellows to identify where each tool belongs in the board workflow. Explain that a configured connector improves continuity and provenance, but does not make a claim more truthful.

Bring one researchable question and, if possible, one article from your discipline.

## Protect sensitive research material

Do not upload material that is:

- confidential or unpublished
- community-identifiable
- restricted by an agreement, license, or ethics protocol

When in doubt, use public sources or a prepared example.

Note:
Pause here for a clear data-safety reminder. Institutional policies and research agreements take priority over convenience. Fellows can complete the activity with the public practice question.

## The intellectual work is still ours

> When an AI tool gives a research answer with citations, the intellectual work is still ours.

| Layer | Human question |
|---|---|
| **Discovery** | What might be relevant? |
| **Annotation** | What do we need to examine, and what does each source actually say? |
| **Synthesis** | Where do sources converge, diverge, and remain silent? |

Note:
Introduce the three layers of human involvement. Refer back to them in the demos and lab. A tool can help at each layer, but the researcher remains responsible for the transition from one layer to the next.

## Three demos, one question

Use the same practice question in every environment.

Compare:

- what each tool interprets as the topic
- what evidence it surfaces
- what information it makes easy to inspect
- what still requires full-text verification

Note:
Keeping the question constant makes differences between tools visible. Ask fellows to note changes in vocabulary, source selection, population, method, and confidence.

---

## Demo 1: General ChatGPT or Claude

```text
Help answer this research question. Identify the population,
intervention or phenomenon, comparison if relevant, outcome,
context, and useful synonyms. Do not claim that studies exist
unless you can provide verifiable sources. Topic: [topic].
```

Note:
Use this prompt to demonstrate question formation and search planning. Treat any literature claims as unverified until they can be traced to a real source.

---

## Compare two forms of evidence

```text
Give me two versions of the question: one causal or
effectiveness question and one exploratory or interpretive
question. Show what evidence each would require.
```

Look for useful framing and plausible but unverified literature claims.

Note:
Compare the two question types as a class. Ask how the framing changes suitable methods, sources, and claims. Point out that an assistant may be strong at question formation even when its source claims need verification.

## Demo 2: Consensus website

1. Enter the focused question
2. Read whether the answer implies strong agreement, mixed findings, or limited evidence
3. Open two cited records
4. Save one candidate source

Do not treat the candidate as evidence for the synthesis yet.

Note:
Use the practice question first, then show one discipline-specific variation. Read the answer language closely and distinguish a candidate citation from a verified source.

## Questions for the Consensus demo

1. Is the tool answering our exact question or a nearby one?
2. Which keywords or concepts does it emphasize?
3. Whose population and context are represented?
4. Is one viewpoint emphasized?
5. What would make us include or exclude this paper?

Note:
Use these questions to slow down the move from discovery to evidence. Invite fellows to propose one inclusion criterion and one exclusion criterion based on the research question.

---

## Demo 3: Consensus inside Codex

```text
Use the Consensus research tool to find up to five studies
relevant to this question: [question]. Return title, year,
population or context, study type, central finding, and a direct
source link. Separate tool-retrieved facts from your interpretation.
```

Note:
Ask the assistant to use the configured research tool explicitly. Show the tool or provenance indicator if the interface provides one. Explain which details came from retrieval and which came from the assistant's interpretation.

---

## Continue the connected workflow

```text
Which two sources are most directly comparable, and what
information must I verify in the full text before combining them?
```

The benefit is workflow continuity, not greater truthfulness.

Note:
Show how the conversation can move from discovery to a reading plan. Do not imply that using Consensus through another interface makes its results more accurate. The original source remains the verification point.

## The synthesis lab

Use a focused question from your discipline or the practice question.

1. Search with at least two tools
2. Apply an inclusion or exclusion criterion
3. Build a small keep list
4. Select one claim for full-text verification
5. Record evidence in the matrix
6. Draft a synthesis memo or presentation outline

Note:
Give fellows 25 to 30 minutes. They may work alone or in pairs. Circulate and ask where they are in the board workflow, what each tool surfaced, and what decision still belongs to them.

---

## First compare the tools

| Comparison | Notes |
|---|---|
| What appeared in both result sets? | |
| What appeared in only one? | |
| How did keywords and framing differ? | |
| Which populations or viewpoints were emphasized? | |
| What will you keep, exclude, or search for next? | |

Note:
Ask fellows to compare the result sets before choosing a source. The purpose is to make tool-specific coverage and blind spots visible, not to count which tool returned more papers.

---

## Verify one claim in the original PDF

Record:

1. The claim you are checking
2. The PDF title and stable link or citation
3. The page number
4. A short supporting passage or accurate paraphrase
5. Whether the source supports, complicates, or contradicts the claim

Note:
Require every fellow or pair to open an original PDF. An abstract or AI summary is not enough for this step. If the full text is unavailable, record the access limit and choose another claim that can be verified.

---

## Build an evidence matrix

| Source | Population and context | Method | Finding | Page and support | Limits or gaps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | | | |
| 2 | | | | | |
| 3 | | | | | |

Note:
The matrix keeps claims connected to sources while preserving important differences. Fellows do not need three fully verified papers during the workshop. They can mark fields that still require reading.

---

## Draft the synthesis

Write a short memo or presentation outline that answers:

1. What appears to help, for whom, and in what context?
2. What does the verified evidence suggest?
3. Where do sources disagree or use different definitions?
4. What does the evidence leave out?
5. What should be read or searched next?

Note:
Ask fellows to distinguish a tentative pattern from a claim they would put into a literature review. The draft should name disagreement and limitations instead of smoothing them away.

---

## Prompts for the lab

```text
Suggest stronger search questions, synonyms, and disciplinary
variations for this topic.

What papers, methods, or populations may be absent from this
result set?

Create a synthesis outline from these verified notes. Label
claims that still need checking.

What disagreements should I examine before describing consensus?
```

Note:
Fellows do not need to use every prompt. Ask them to select the prompt that addresses the weakest part of their workflow. Remind them to provide verified notes rather than asking a model to infer from citations alone.

---

## Share and critique

1. Which tool seemed most convincing, and why?
2. What would have been easy to miss with only one tool?
3. Which claim did you verify, and what changed after reading the PDF?
4. Where did the sources disagree or remain silent?
5. Where did a tool help you think better?
6. Where did it tempt you to stop thinking?

Note:
During the share-out, compare review habits as much as results. Invite fellows to explain how they checked a claim, noticed a gap, or changed their question.

---

## What remains human?

Framing the question. Selecting evidence. Reading the original source. Understanding context. Interpreting disagreement. Naming limitations. Taking responsibility for the claims you write.

**AI can accelerate a literature review. It cannot replace disciplinary judgment.**

Note:
Close by asking what responsible synthesis requires when tools can summarize papers faster than people can read them. Ask each fellow to name one workflow step they will adopt and one check they will not delegate.
